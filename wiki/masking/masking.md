# Masking

> The conscious or unconscious act of hiding, suppressing, or changing one's neurodivergent traits to fit into neurotypical social environments.

## Overview

Masking — also called camouflaging, compensation, or social passing — is what happens when a neurodivergent person learns to perform neurotypical behavior. It can look like forcing eye contact during conversations, scripting out what to say before speaking, laughing at the right moment even when you're not sure what's funny, suppressing stimming, or mirroring the body language and speech patterns of the people around you.

Masking is not a choice in the clean sense of the word. For most neurodivergent people, it develops gradually and unconsciously — a survival adaptation shaped by years of negative feedback for being "too much," "too intense," "too weird," or "not getting it." By adulthood, many people don't even know they're doing it. It has become so automatic it feels like who they are.

Research distinguishes between two dimensions of masking: *behavioral camouflaging* (what others can observe — reduced stimming, scripted social responses, mimicry) and *self-perceived camouflaging* (the internal felt sense of suppressing one's authentic self). Both matter, and they don't always line up — someone can appear unmasked while still feeling profoundly hidden.

## How It Shows Up in Real Life

- Preparing scripts before phone calls, social events, or difficult conversations
- Studying people closely to mirror their humor, pacing, and tone
- Suppressing stimming (rocking, hand-flapping, fidgeting) in public but releasing it alone at home
- Forcing or rehearsing eye contact despite it feeling unnatural or overwhelming
- Laughing along when you didn't process the joke
- Saying "I'm fine" reflexively when you're not, because explaining the real answer feels too complicated
- Performing extroversion all day, then crashing completely when alone (*autistic burnout*)
- Having a "work self" that feels nothing like your actual self
- Exhaustion that seems disproportionate to the day's activities

## Why It Happens (Mechanism)

Masking develops in response to social feedback. When neurodivergent traits attract negative reactions — ridicule, correction, exclusion, or punishment — the brain learns to suppress those traits in social contexts. This is a real form of social learning, and for many people it starts in early childhood, long before they have any framework for understanding why they're different.

For autistic individuals, research consistently links higher levels of camouflaging to worse mental health outcomes: greater anxiety, depression, and suicidality ([Hull et al., 2021, systematic review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34563942/)). A 2024 PMC study found that masking is associated with lower self-esteem, higher interpersonal trauma, reduced authenticity, and reduced psychological wellbeing ([PMC11317797](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11317797/)).

For people with ADHD, masking often involves suppressing impulsivity in social settings, performing attentiveness (eye contact, nodding) while the mind is elsewhere, or working enormous effort to appear organized and on top of things when internally things are chaotic.

Masking is also significantly gendered. Research repeatedly finds that women and girls, and people assigned female at birth, mask more than men and boys — partly because of socialization pressures to be agreeable, readable, and relationally skilled. This is one reason why ADHD and autism are so frequently missed or misdiagnosed in women ([Frick et al., 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11956369/)).

## Common Misconceptions

**Myth: Masking means faking it — so the person isn't really that different.**
Reality: Masking is a learned performance built on top of a real neurological difference. The traits don't disappear when masked — they're suppressed at significant cognitive and emotional cost.

**Myth: If someone can mask, they don't need support.**
Reality: Masking is metabolically expensive. The ability to mask in some contexts does not mean a person is "high-functioning" or doesn't need accommodations. It means they've learned to hide their needs, often until they can't anymore.

**Myth: Masking is a choice people can just stop making.**
Reality: For many people, masking is deeply automated. Unmasking requires sustained, conscious work, and often a safe environment to do it in. See [[unmasking]].

**Myth: Masking only applies to autism.**
Reality: Masking is documented across neurodivergent conditions, including ADHD. The research base is strongest for autism, but the mechanism — suppressing genuine traits to avoid social penalty — is not autism-specific.

## Coping & Navigation

### In Neuroaffirming Spaces
- Practice dropping one mask behavior at a time in environments where you feel genuinely safe. This could be stimming freely, saying "I don't know" instead of bluffing, or leaving a social situation when you need to rather than enduring it.
- Work with a neurodiversity-affirming therapist to identify which behaviors are masked versus which are genuinely yours. See [Therapist Neurodiversity Collective](https://therapistndc.org/neurodiversity-affirming-therapy/).
- Community helps. Being around other neurodivergent people often reduces the effort of masking significantly — not because you're trying to unmask, but because there's simply less need.

### In Neurotypical or Mixed Spaces
- Identify your highest-cost masks — the ones that drain you most — and deprioritize those where possible, even if you keep others.
- Build in recovery time after high-masking events (meetings, parties, long social days). The crash is real and physiological.
- Selective disclosure of your neurodivergent identity can reduce the felt pressure to mask in specific relationships. See [[self-disclosure]].
- Notice the difference between *adapting* (adjusting communication style, which has legitimate social value) and *suppressing* (hiding who you are, which carries mental health costs). The former is a skill; the latter is a toll.
